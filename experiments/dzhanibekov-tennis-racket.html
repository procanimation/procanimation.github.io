In 1985, aboard the Soviet space station Salyut 7, cosmonaut Vladimir Dzhanibekov observed an astonishing phenomenon: a wing nut spinning off a bolt floated freely in zero gravity, rotating smoothly—until it suddenly executed a spontaneous 180-degree flip in mid-air, reversed its orientation, and kept spinning, repeating this cycle indefinitely. For years classified by Soviet authorities, this behavior is a pure consequence of classical mechanics: the Intermediate Axis Theorem (or Tennis Racket Theorem). When a rigid body has three unequal moments of inertia ($I_1 > I_2 > I_3$), rotation about the largest and smallest axes is stable, but rotation about the intermediate axis is dynamically unstable, governed by a hyperbolic saddle separatrix across its phase space.

The Rigid Body Dynamics Lexicon

Euler's Equations of Motion (1758)

In torque-free rotation, the components of angular velocity couple through nonlinear inertial products: $I_1 \dot{\omega}_1 = (I_2 - I_3)\omega_2 \omega_3$, $I_2 \dot{\omega}_2 = (I_3 - I_1)\omega_3 \omega_1$, and $I_3 \dot{\omega}_3 = (I_1 - I_2)\omega_1 \omega_2$.

The Tennis Racket Theorem

Toss a tennis racket spinning around its handle (minimum inertia axis $I_3$) or its face normal (maximum axis $I_1$): the rotation is stable. But spin it around its face width (intermediate axis $I_2$), and any microscopic perturbation grows exponentially ($\lambda^2 > 0$), forcing an unavoidable half-twist flip.

Poinsot's Inertia Ellipsoid & Polhodes

Louis Poinsot (1834) visualized rotation through the intersection of two conserved surfaces: the angular momentum sphere ($L^2 = \text{const}$) and the kinetic energy ellipsoid ($2E = \text{const}$). The resulting intersection curves—Polhodes—form closed stable loops around axes 1 and 3, but cross in a figure-eight saddle separatrix at axis 2.

Celestial & Aerospace Tumbling

The Dzhanibekov effect governs the chaotic tumbling of Saturn's irregular moon Hyperion, tumbling interstellar asteroids like 'Oumuamua, and attitude control in satellites (e.g. the 1958 instability of America's Explorer 1 satellite).
