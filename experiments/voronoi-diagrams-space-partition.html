In 1908, Ukrainian mathematician Georgy Voronoy formalized a profound geometric question: given a discrete set of seed points scattered across space, how does space naturally divide among them? The answer is the Voronoi Diagram (also known as Dirichlet tessellation or Thiessen polygons): every seed claims the convex territory containing all points closer to it than to any other seed. When seeds expand like circular shockwaves, their boundaries form straight bisectors. Its mathematical dual—the Delaunay Triangulation—connects seeds whose territories touch, maximizing the minimum angle of triangles to eliminate skinny slivers. From the venation of dragonfly wings and giraffe patches to cell biology and the colossal cosmic voids of astrophysics, nature computes Voronoi diagrams everywhere.

The Geometric Partitioning Lexicon

The Voronoi Cell ($V(p_i)$)

Given a metric space $(X, d)$ and a set of distinct seed points $P = \{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n\}$, the Voronoi cell associated with site $p_i$ is defined as: $$V(p_i) = \{ x \in X \mid d(x, p_i) \le d(x, p_j) \; \forall j \ne i \}$$ In Euclidean space ($\mathbb{R}^2$), each cell is the convex intersection of $n-1$ half-planes formed by perpendicular bisectors.

Delaunay Triangulation Dual ($\mathcal{DT}(P)$)

Discovered by Boris Delaunay in 1934, the Delaunay triangulation is the planar graph dual to the Voronoi diagram. An edge connects sites $p_i$ and $p_j$ if and only if their Voronoi cells share a common boundary: $$\mathcal{DT}(P) = \{ (p_i, p_j) \mid V(p_i) \cap V(p_j) \ne \emptyset \}$$ It is the unique triangulation that avoids narrow sliver triangles by maximizing the minimum interior angle.

The Empty Circumcircle Criterion

Three sites $p_i, p_j, p_k$ form a Delaunay triangle if and only if the circumcircle passing through all three points contains no other seed site in its interior. The center of this circumcircle is exactly the triple-junction Voronoi vertex where the three cells meet.

Centroidal Voronoi & Lloyd's Relaxation

In a Centroidal Voronoi Tessellation (CVT), each seed site coincides exactly with the geometric center of mass (centroid) of its cell: $$p_i = C_i = \frac{\int_{V(p_i)} x \, \rho(x) \, dx}{\int_{V(p_i)} \rho(x) \, dx}$$ Iteratively moving sites to their cell centroids (Lloyd's algorithm) causes irregular random cells to relax into hexagonal honeycomb packings—the exact minimal-energy surface configuration seen in soap froth and biological epithelia.
