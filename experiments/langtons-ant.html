In 1986, artificial life pioneer Christopher Langton conceived a two-dimensional cellular automaton that demonstrates the profound boundary between chaos and order: Langton's Ant. Living on an infinite grid of black and white cells, the ant follows just two local rules. Despite its absolute simplicity, it passes through distinct evolutionary epochs: beginning with simple bilateral symmetry, plunging into a swirling chaotic pseudo-random walk for over $10,000$ steps, and then spontaneously organizing into an infinite 104-step diagonal highway.

The Emergence Lexicon

The Elementary Ruleset

On a white tile: turn $90^\circ$ clockwise (right), flip the tile's color to black, and step forward 1 cell. On a black tile: turn $90^\circ$ counter-clockwise (left), flip the tile's color to white, and step forward 1 cell.

The Chaotic Epoch (Steps 200–10,000)

For roughly $10,000$ steps, the ant generates an aperiodic, pseudo-random cloud of black and white tiles. Its trajectories exhibit statistical properties indistinguishable from random walks, erasing its initial symmetrical origin.

The 104-Step Highway Emergence

Around step $10,400$, the ant builds a recurrent sequence of 104 steps that shifts its position by $(+2, -2)$ relative to the grid. It continues to march indefinitely along this diagonal highway, never escaping it on an empty grid.

Cohen-Kung Unbounded Theorem

In 1993, mathematicians proved that the trajectory of Langton's ant is strictly unbounded: regardless of the initial configuration of black cells on a finite grid, the ant cannot remain trapped in a bounded region forever.
