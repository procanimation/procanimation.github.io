Everything in the universe moves, from galaxies hurtling through deep cosmic voids to harmonic crystal vibrations. Before we can explain why bodies move through forces and energy, we must first master the exact language of motion itself: kinematics. Formulated geometrically by Galileo Galilei and unified with calculus by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, one-dimensional kinematics describes the position $x(t)$, instantaneous velocity $v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}$, and acceleration $a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt}$ of an object along a spatial line.

The Architectural Trinity of Kinematics

1. Displacement vs Distance

$$\Delta x = x(t_2) - x(t_1)$$. Displacement is a vector describing net change in position, while distance $$s = \int |v(t)| \, dt$$ is the scalar accumulated path length.

2. The Geometric Derivative ($v = \frac{dx}{dt}$)

Average velocity $$\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ is the secant slope. In the limit $$\Delta t \to 0$$, it becomes the tangent line: $$v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t+\Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t}$$.

3. Acceleration as Curvature ($a = \frac{dv}{dt}$)

Acceleration is the concavity of the position curve: $$a(t) = \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}$$. When $$v$$ and $$a$$ share the same sign, speed increases; when opposing, the body decelerates.

4. Galileo's Uniform Acceleration Laws

Under constant acceleration, area under $$v(t)$$ yields $$\Delta x = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}a t^2$$. Starting from rest, distances in equal time intervals follow the ratio $$1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : 9$$.

1. The Foundations of 1D Kinematics

In classical physics, motion is described without reference to the forces causing it. Given a particle moving along the real line $\mathbb{R}$, its position is specified by the real-valued function $x(t)$.

The Velocity Tangent
$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t + \Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t}$$

Geometrically, the average velocity $\bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$ is the slope of the secant line intersecting two points on the position curve. As $\Delta t \to 0$, the secant collapses into the unique tangent line at time $t$.

2. Acceleration & The Second Derivative

Acceleration is the instantaneous rate of change of velocity:

$$a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}$$

On a position-time graph, acceleration represents curvature:

  • $\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} > 0$: The curve is concave upward. If $v > 0$, the body is speeding up; if $v < 0$, it is decelerating.
  • $\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} < 0$: The curve is concave downward.
  • $\frac{d^2 x}{dt^2} = 0$: Inflection point where acceleration vanishes and velocity achieves a local extreme value.

3. Galileo's Kinematic Laws of Uniform Acceleration

When acceleration is constant ($a = \text{const}$), integration yields the classic kinematic equations:

$$v(t) = v_0 + a t$$ $$\Delta x = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2$$ $$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a \Delta x$$

Under free fall from rest ($v_0 = 0$), Galileo discovered that the distance traversed in successive equal time intervals obeys the ratio of odd integers:

$$\Delta x_1 : \Delta x_2 : \Delta x_3 : \Delta x_4 = 1 : 3 : 5 : 7$$
