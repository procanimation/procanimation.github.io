In 1833, Irish mathematician and physicist William Rowan Hamilton achieved one of the most profound conceptual revolutions in the history of science. Moving beyond Newton's vector forces and Lagrange's generalized velocities, Hamilton introduced conjugate momentum $p = \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \dot{q}$ via a Legendre transformation, projecting dynamical systems into a $2n$-dimensional space of breathtaking geometric purity: Phase Space. In this symplectic manifold, the state of an entire physical system is a single point, its time evolution is governed by canonical flow equations, and an ensemble of states behaves as an ideal, incompressible fluid whose volume is strictly conserved by Liouville's Theorem.

The Symplectic Mechanics Lexicon

The Legendre Transformation

Lagrangian mechanics describes physics via generalized coordinates and velocities $(q, \dot{q})$ with $\mathcal{L} = T - V$. Hamilton defined momentum $p = \partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \dot{q}$ and replaced velocity through the Legendre transform $H(q, p) = p\dot{q} - \mathcal{L}$, formulating physics as total energy on cotangent bundles $T^*M$.

Hamilton's Canonical Equations

Physical state points evolve according to the coupled first-order system $\dot{q} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}$ and $\dot{p} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}$. The skew-symmetric minus sign reflects the non-Euclidean symplectic gradient governed by the differential two-form $\Omega = dq \wedge dp$.

Liouville's Phase Fluid Incompressibility (1838)

The divergence of the Hamiltonian vector field is identically zero: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}_H = \frac{\partial \dot{q}}{\partial q} + \frac{\partial \dot{p}}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q \partial p} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p \partial q} \equiv 0$. Phase space volume is perfectly conserved; information can never be destroyed.

The Separatrix & KAM Chaos

In nonlinear systems like the pendulum, the separatrix ($E = 1$) separates closed librational orbits from unbounded rotations. Under external periodic perturbations, the Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser (KAM) theorem proves invariant tori break down near resonances into Henri Poincaré's chaotic homoclinic tangles.

The Symplectic Architecture of Classical Mechanics

Isaac Newton's second law $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ operates directly in Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^3$, where acceleration couples to second-order differential equations. In 1788, Joseph-Louis Lagrange shifted the paradigm to the tangent bundle $TM$ parameterized by generalized coordinates $q^i$ and velocities $\dot{q}^i$, with the stationary action principle $\delta \int \mathcal{L} \, dt = 0$.

Hamilton completed this abstraction by transitioning from velocities $\dot{q}$ to canonical conjugate momenta $p_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}^i}$. Under the Legendre transform:

$$H(q, p, t) = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \dot{q}^i - \mathcal{L}(q, \dot{q}, t)$$

Hamilton replaced $n$ second-order differential equations with $2n$ coupled first-order canonical equations:

$$\dot{q}^i = +\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}$$

Liouville's Theorem & Symplectic Incompressibility

Consider an ensemble of possible initial states occupying a volume $\Gamma_0$ in phase space. The velocity field in phase space is $\mathbf{v} = (\dot{\mathbf{q}}, \dot{\mathbf{p}})$. Calculating the divergence of this vector field yields:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{\partial \dot{q}^i}{\partial q^i} + \frac{\partial \dot{p}_i}{\partial p_i} \right) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q^i \partial p_i} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_i \partial q^i} \right) \equiv 0$$

Because the mixed partial derivatives of $H$ commute by Clairaut's theorem, the divergence is strictly zero. Hence, by the divergence theorem, the phase space volume is an invariant of motion:

$$\frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Gamma(t)} dq \, dp = 0$$

Even when a phase droplet shears, twists, and stretches into an ultra-thin filament under nonlinear or chaotic dynamics, its total multi-dimensional volume never changes. Information in classical mechanics is eternal and indestructible.
