In 47 AD, during the Siege of Yodfat, Jewish historian Flavius Josephus and forty fellow soldiers were trapped in a cave by Roman legions. Refusing capture, the group chose mutual suicide: standing in a circle, counting off, and eliminating every second person until only one remained. Josephus—preferring survival—calculated the exact position to stand and became the sole survivor. For two millennia, the Josephus Problem stood as an intriguing combinatorial riddle. In modern computer science, it reveals a breathtakingly beautiful connection between powers of two and a single cyclic binary bit shift.

The Combinatorial & Bitwise Elimination Lexicon

The Classical Elimination Rule

A circle of $n$ people are numbered $1$ to $n$. Counting begins at position $1$. Every second person ($k = 2$) is eliminated: person $2$ is eliminated first, then $4$, then $6$, continuing around the loop until only one person survives, designated $W(n)$.

The Power-of-Two Invariance ($n = 2^k$)

Whenever the population $n$ is an exact power of two ($2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\dots$), a complete first pass around the circle eliminates every even-numbered person, exactly halving the population while leaving person $1$ with the sword again. By induction, person #1 always survives: $$W(2^k) = 1$$

The General Closed-Form: $W(n) = 2l + 1$

Any positive integer $n$ can be uniquely written as $n = 2^m + l$, where $2^m$ is the largest power of two less than or equal to $n$, and $l$ is the remainder ($0 \le l < 2^m$). Eliminating $l$ people reduces the remaining group to an exact power of two ($2^m$). The person holding the turn after $l$ eliminations is: $$W(n) = 2l + 1$$ For Josephus' original circle of $n = 41$: $41 = 32 + 9 \implies W(41) = 2(9) + 1 = \mathbf{19}$.

The 1-Bit Cyclic Shift Magic

Represent $n$ in binary: $n = 1 b_{m-1} b_{m-2} \dots b_1 b_0$. Because $2l + 1$ corresponds to shifting the bits of $l$ left by one and setting the least significant bit to $1$, the survivor position is obtained by taking the leading Most Significant Bit (MSB) and cycling it to the end: $$W(n) = \text{RotateLeft}(n)$$ For $n = 41$: $41 = 101001_2 \implies \text{Rotate}(101001_2) = 010011_2 = \mathbf{19}$. A single CPU instruction solves the life-or-death problem!
