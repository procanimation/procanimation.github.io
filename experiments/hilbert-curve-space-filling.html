In 1890, Italian mathematician Giuseppe Peano rocked the foundations of mathematics by proving that a one-dimensional curve could be continuous and surjective—meaning a zero-thickness 1D line could pass through every single point of a 2D square. A year later in 1891, David Hilbert published an extraordinarily elegant geometric construction: the Hilbert Curve. By recursively dividing a square into four quadrants and connecting them in a rotated U-shaped motif, the Hilbert curve weaves an infinite labyrinth that fills the plane with a Hausdorff dimension of exactly $D = 2.0$.

The Space-Filling & Fractal Lexicon

Continuous & Surjective Mapping

A space-filling curve is a continuous mapping $f: [0, 1] \to [0, 1]^2$ whose image fills the entire two-dimensional unit square. By Cantor's cardinality theorem, both the unit interval and unit square contain the exact same infinite cardinality of points ($2^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$).

Hausdorff Dimension ($D = 2.0$)

At each iteration $k$, the scale factor shrinks by $r = 1/2$, while the number of self-similar segments increases by $N = 4$. The fractal Hausdorff dimension is: $$D = \frac{\log N}{\log(1/r)} = \frac{\log 4}{\log 2} = 2.000$$ Because $D = 2$, the 1D curve completely occupies two-dimensional Euclidean area.

Infinite Path Length Divergence

At order $k$, the grid resolution is $2^k \times 2^k$ and contains $4^k$ points. The total Euclidean path length is $L_k = 2^k - \frac{1}{2^k}$. As $k \to \infty$, the length diverges to infinity ($L_k \to \infty$), yet it remains strictly bounded inside the finite unit square.

Locality-Preserving Spatial Hashing

Unlike simple raster row-scanning or Z-order (Morton) curves, the Hilbert curve minimizes jump discontinuities. Points that are close along the 1D Hilbert index $d$ are guaranteed to be close in 2D Euclidean space. This locality makes Hilbert indexing the industry standard for GIS mapping (Google S2), spatial databases, and GPU cache locality.
