In 1952, two years before his tragic death, British mathematician and computing pioneer Alan Turing published a revolutionary paper titled "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society. He posed a question that had baffled biologists for centuries: How does a homogeneous, spherically symmetric cluster of identical embryonic cells know where to grow stripes, tentacles, or limbs? Turing proved that symmetry can break spontaneously through the simple interaction of two diffusing chemical substances.

The Reaction-Diffusion Lexicon

Activator vs. Inhibitor

Morphogenesis requires two chemical morphogens: an activator ($u$) that catalyzes its own creation, and an inhibitor ($v$) that suppresses the activator.

Diffusion Asymmetry

Turing's crucial insight: the inhibitor must diffuse through tissue significantly faster than the activator ($D_v \gg D_u$). This local activation and lateral inhibition forms stable periodic boundaries.

Feed & Kill Kinetics

In the Gray-Scott formulation, parameter $F$ represents the constant rate at which raw reactant $A$ is replenished, while $k$ represents the decay rate of reactant $B$.

Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking

A mathematically uniform field is unstable. Microscopic thermal fluctuations are amplified into macroscopic, macroscopic animal coat patterns: cheetah spots, zebra stripes, and angelfish bands.

The Non-Linear Partial Differential Equations

The Gray-Scott model simulates a coupled pair of non-linear partial differential equations governed by spatial diffusion (the 2D Laplacian operator $\nabla^2$) and cubic chemical kinetics ($uv^2$):

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = D_u \nabla^2 u - uv^2 + F(1 - u)$$ $$\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = D_v \nabla^2 v + uv^2 - (F + k)v$$

Where $D_u = 1.0$ and $D_v = 0.5$. Because $D_v$ is strictly smaller than $D_u$, the activator diffuses much slower than the surrounding inhibitor, confining reactions to tight geometric clusters. As John Pearson classified in his famous 1993 Science paper, small variations in the 2D parameter space $(F, k)$ produce a vast zoo of natural textures—from isolated solitons to replicating waves.
