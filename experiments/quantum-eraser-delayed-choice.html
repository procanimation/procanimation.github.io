In 1801, Thomas Young proved light travels as a wave. Over a century later, quantum mechanics revealed that individual photons fired one by one still build up an interference pattern. Yet observing which path the photon takes destroys the wave pattern instantaneously, collapsing it into two classical clumps. In 1978, John Archibald Wheeler asked: what if we delay the choice of measurement until after the photon has already passed through the slits? In 1999, Kim et al. built the definitive laboratory experiment using entangled photons, demonstrating that path information can be erased 8 nanoseconds after the primary signal photon has already been absorbed. Does the future rewrite the past?

The Quantum Eraser & Measurement Lexicon

Wavefunction Interference vs Which-Way Knowledge

In standard double-slit superposition, the probability density on the detector screen includes an interference cross-term: $$I(y) = |\psi_A + \psi_B|^2 = |\psi_A|^2 + |\psi_B|^2 + 2 \text{Re}(\psi_A^* \psi_B)$$ When the path becomes distinguishable by an observer, the cross-term vanishes ($2 \text{Re}(\psi_A^* \psi_B) = 0$), leaving a classical sum of two separate clumps.

Wheeler's Cosmic Interferometer (1978)

Wheeler envisioned light from a quasar billions of light years away bent around a massive galaxy by gravitational lensing along two cosmic routes. An astronomer on Earth can choose to point two telescopes (particle path detection) or insert a beam splitter (wave interference). The decision is made billions of years after the light traversed the galaxy.

The Scully-Drühl Polarization Eraser (1982)

Marlan Scully and Kai Drühl showed that physical disturbance is not required to destroy interference. Mark slit A with left-circular polarization $|L\rangle$ and slit B with right-circular polarization $|R\rangle$. Because $\langle L | R \rangle = 0$, paths are distinguishable and fringes vanish. Placing a $45^\circ$ diagonal polarizer $|D\rangle = (|L\rangle + |R\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ erases the path marker, instantly restoring interference!

Spontaneous Parametric Down-Conversion (SPDC)

In the 1999 Kim et al. experiment, an argon-ion UV laser pumps a nonlinear Beta Barium Borate (BBO) crystal. A single high-energy photon splits into an entangled pair: $$|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( |A\rangle_s |A\rangle_i + |B\rangle_s |B\rangle_i \right)$$ The Signal photon ($s$) travels to primary detector $D_0$, while the Idler photon ($i$) enters an optical maze.

The 8-Nanosecond Optical Delay Loop

The Signal path to $D_0$ is only 1.3 meters long, while the Idler path to detectors $D_1$ through $D_4$ is 3.6 meters long. The Signal photon is absorbed at $D_0$ approximately 7.7 nanoseconds before the Idler photon even reaches the beam splitters that decide whether its path is preserved or erased!

The No-Communication Theorem & Anti-Phase Fringes

The experiment does not permit retrocausality or backward-in-time messaging. Looking at $D_0$ alone shows a completely smooth, featureless Gaussian curve. The interference pattern from eraser detector $D_1$ is matched by an anti-phase pattern at $D_2$ shifted by $180^\circ$ ($\pi$). Summed together: $$P(D_0) = P(D_0 | D_1) + P(D_0 | D_2) = \text{Smooth Blur}$$ Interference can only be seen through classical coincidence counters, strictly bounded by the speed of light.
