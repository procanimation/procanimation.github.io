Can dead-simple deterministic rules on a two-dimensional grid give birth to living, self-replicating virtual ecosystems? In October 1970, British mathematician John Horton Conway published the Game of Life in Martin Gardner's Scientific American column. With zero players, no physics engine, and just four transition rules, the system exhibits unbounded emergent complexity—proving that the highest orders of life and computation can arise from the simplest axioms.

The Cellular Automata Lexicon

Moore Neighborhood

The 8 cells horizontally, vertically, and diagonally adjacent to any coordinate $(x,y)$. All life decisions depend solely on the sum of live neighbors: $N \in [0, 8]$.

Rule B3/S23

B3 (Birth): A dead cell becomes alive if exactly 3 neighbors exist. S23 (Survival): A live cell survives if it has 2 or 3 neighbors; otherwise it dies of underpopulation or overpopulation.

The Gosper Glider Gun

Discovered by Bill Gosper at MIT in November 1970. A period-30 oscillator that continuously produces traveling gliders, proving finite initial patterns can yield infinite population growth.

Turing Completeness

Because streams of gliders can collide to create logic gates (AND, OR, NOT) and memory registers, Conway's Game of Life is a universal computer capable of simulating any computable algorithm.
