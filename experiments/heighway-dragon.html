In 1966, NASA physicists John Heighway, Bruce Banks, and William Harter were experimenting with simple paper strips. They discovered that folding a strip of paper in half repeatedly in the same direction, and then unfolding every crease to a 90-degree angle, produces an extraordinarily intricate fractal beast: the Heighway Dragon. Despite never crossing itself, its perimeter doubles with every fold, eventually filling the two-dimensional plane completely.

The Heighway Dragon Lexicon

The Paper-Folding Sequence

A strip folded $n$ times contains $2^n - 1$ creases. The sequence of Left ($L$) and Right ($R$) turns satisfies the recurrence: $S_n = S_{n-1} + R + \text{reverse}(\text{invert}(S_{n-1}))$. At every step, the center crease is always a Right fold.

Lindenmayer System (L-System)

In formal language theory, the curve is generated by alphabet $\{F, +, -\}$, axiom $FX$, and parallel production rules: $X \to X + YF+$ and $Y \to -FX - Y$. Here $F$ moves forward, $+$ turns right 90°, and $-$ turns left 90°.

Hausdorff Boundary Dimension

While the interior of the dragon curve has a topological Hausdorff dimension of $d = 2$ (it tile-covers a solid 2D region), its jagged fractal boundary has dimension $d_H \approx 1.523627$, root of the equation $2^{d_H/2} + 2^{d_H} = \dots$.

The 4-Dragon Tessellation

Four identical Heighway dragons sharing a single central origin point and rotated by 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270° interlock seamlessly like jigsaw puzzle pieces, tiling the infinite two-dimensional Cartesian plane without overlap or empty gaps.
