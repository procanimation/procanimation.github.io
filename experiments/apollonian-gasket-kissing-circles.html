In 200 BC, Greek geometer Apollonius of Perga formulated a classic geometric challenge: given three mutually tangent ("kissing") circles, how can one construct a fourth circle tangent to all three? For nearly two millennia, the problem was treated as an isolated Euclidean puzzle. Then in 1643, French philosopher and mathematician René Descartes discovered an extraordinary algebraic relation: Descartes' Circle Theorem. By defining the "bend" or curvature of each circle as the reciprocal of its radius ($k = 1/r$), Descartes proved that four mutually kissing circles satisfy a quadratic relationship: the square of the sum of their curvatures equals twice the sum of the squares of their curvatures. Packing new tangent circles recursively into every curvilinear triangular void produces the breathtaking, infinitely intricate Apollonian Gasket.

The Circle Packing & Curvature Lexicon

Curvature Definition ($k = 1/r$)

In Apollonian geometry, every circle is characterized by its curvature (or bend) $k = 1/r$. For internally tangent circles (such as the outer enclosing boundary that surrounds the others), the curvature is defined with a negative sign ($k_{\text{outer}} = -1/R$). A straight line is simply a circle of infinite radius with zero curvature ($k = 0$).

Descartes' Circle Theorem (1643)

If four circles are mutually tangent at six distinct points, their curvatures $k_1, k_2, k_3, k_4$ satisfy: $$(k_1 + k_2 + k_3 + k_4)^2 = 2 \left(k_1^2 + k_2^2 + k_3^2 + k_4^2\right)$$ Given three kissing circles, solving for the fourth yields two conjugate solutions: $$k_4 = k_1 + k_2 + k_3 \pm 2 \sqrt{k_1 k_2 + k_2 k_3 + k_3 k_1}$$

The Complex Descartes Theorem

To determine the exact spatial coordinates $(x_4, y_4)$ of the newly generated circle, the centers are treated as points in the complex plane $z = x + i y$: $$k_4 z_4 = k_1 z_1 + k_2 z_2 + k_3 z_3 \pm 2 \sqrt{k_1 k_2 z_1 z_2 + k_2 k_3 z_2 z_3 + k_3 k_1 z_3 z_1}$$ This allows exact, closed-form computation of all recursive circle coordinates without numerical approximation.

Integral Apollonian Packings & Soddy's "Kiss Precise"

In 1936, Nobel laureate chemist Frederick Soddy rediscovered Descartes' theorem and published a famous poem, "The Kiss Precise," in Nature. When the initial four curvatures are integers (e.g. $[-1, 2, 2, 3]$), every subsequent recursive circle throughout the infinite packing inherits an exact integer curvature. This connects Apollonian gaskets to deep Diophantine equations, modular forms, and hyperbolic 3-manifolds.
