In 1865, Scottish mathematical physicist James Clerk Maxwell published a monumental treatise that forever reshaped our comprehension of the physical cosmos: A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field. Prior to Maxwell, static electricity, lodestone magnetism, and optical light were cataloged as isolated phenomena of nature. Maxwell synthesized them into four vector differential equations, introduced the displacement current to preserve charge conservation, and derived that light is a self-sustaining transverse electromagnetic disturbance propagating at $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}$.

The Electrodynamic Architecture

Gauss's Law for Electricity

$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \rho / \varepsilon_0$. Electric charges act as physical sources and sinks. The net flux through any closed Gaussian surface measures the enclosed charge.

Gauss's Law for Magnetism

$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$. Magnetic field lines form continuous unbroken loops. In classical physics, isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist.

Faraday's Law of Induction

$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$. A time-varying magnetic field induces curling electric vortex rings. The negative sign (Lenz's Law) enforces conservation of energy.

The Ampère-Maxwell Law

$\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$. Changing electric fields generate circulating magnetic fields across vacuum, bridging the continuity gap.

1. The Four Classical Equations of Electrodynamics

In microscopic form (Heaviside-Lorentz vector representation), Maxwell’s equations govern the evolution of the electric field $\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ and magnetic field $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}, t)$:

Maxwell's Microscopic Differential Equations
$$\begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} &= \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} && \text{(Gauss's Law)} \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0 && \text{(Gauss's Law for Magnetism)} \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{E} &= -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} && \text{(Faraday's Law of Induction)} \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{B} &= \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} && \text{(Ampère-Maxwell Law)} \end{aligned}$$

2. Derivation of the Electromagnetic Wave Equation

In free space (charge density $\rho = 0$, current density $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{0}$), taking the curl of Faraday's Law and applying the vector calculus identity $\nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) = \nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E}) - \nabla^2 \mathbf{E}$:

$$\nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla \times \mathbf{B})$$

Substituting $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0$ and the vacuum Ampère-Maxwell Law $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$:

$$-\nabla^2 \mathbf{E} = -\mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2} \implies \nabla^2 \mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2}$$

where the wave propagation velocity is identically:

$$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} \approx 2.99792458 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$$
