In 1983, theoretical physicist Stephen Wolfram systematically explored Elementary Cellular Automata (ECA): 1D grids of binary cells whose states evolve in discrete steps based purely on their own value and their two immediate neighbors. With only $2^3 = 8$ possible input neighborhoods, exactly $2^8 = 256$ distinct elementary rules exist. Despite this simplicity, these rules span the entirety of dynamical systems: from trivial extinction to self-similar fractals, genuine cryptographic pseudo-randomness, and universal Turing-complete computation.

The Cellular Automata Lexicon

Rule 30: Aperiodic Chaos ($\text{Class 3}$)

Using the local Boolean function $p \oplus (q \lor r)$, Rule 30 generates non-repeating, deterministic chaos from a single active cell. Its central column passes rigorous statistical randomness tests and served as Mathematica's core pseudo-random number generator.

Rule 90: Bitwise Sierpinski ($\text{Class 2}$)

Computed by $p \oplus r$ (ignoring the center cell), Rule 90 is isomorphic to Pascal's triangle modulo 2. It produces an exact recursive Sierpinski triangle with fractional Hausdorff dimension $D = \frac{\ln 3}{\ln 2} \approx 1.585$.

Rule 110: Turing Completeness ($\text{Class 4}$)

Computed by $(q \land \neg p) \lor (q \oplus r)$. Proven by Matthew Cook in 2004 to be Turing-complete. Localized periodic structures called "gliders" collide and interact, enabling universal algorithmic computation.

Rule 184: Traffic & Particle Annihilation

A deterministic model for vehicular traffic flow and surface deposition. Particles move right if the space ahead is empty, reproducing fluid shockwaves, jam propagation, and ballistic annihilation dynamics.
