In 1988, British mathematician Michael Barnsley proved one of the most counter-intuitive theorems in modern dynamics: the Chaos Game. By repeatedly choosing one of a few affine matrix transformations at random and plotting the sequence of points, an attractor emerges that is completely deterministic, infinitely detailed, and immune to initial conditions. Pure chance, when constrained by contractive mappings, acts as a sculptor of mathematical nature.

The Stochastic Mathematics Lexicon

The Chaos Game Principle

Given a set of $N$ contraction mappings $\{f_1, f_2, \dots, f_N\}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with associated probabilities $p_i$, iterating $P_{n+1} = f_k(P_n)$ with probability $p_k$ generates a sequence whose closure is the unique compact attractor $A = \bigcup_{i=1}^N f_i(A)$ by the Banach Fixed-Point Theorem.

Sierpinski Gasket Convergence

With 3 vertices of an equilateral triangle and uniform jumping $P_{n+1} = \frac{P_n + V_k}{2}$ ($p_k = 1/3$), the points avoid the central inverted triangles completely, converging to a Hausdorff dimension of $D = \frac{\ln 3}{\ln 2} \approx 1.585$.

Barnsley Fern Affine Matrices

Four 2D affine transformations $f_i(x, y) = A_i \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_i$ simulate botanical self-similarity: stem growth ($1\%$), successive main leaflets ($85\%$), left sub-fronds ($7\%$), and right sub-fronds ($7\%$). Its fractal dimension is $D \approx 1.78$.

Hutchinson Operator & Invariance

The attractor is strictly invariant under the union of its transformations. The random walk visits the dense set of points on the fractal with a natural ergodic probability measure.
