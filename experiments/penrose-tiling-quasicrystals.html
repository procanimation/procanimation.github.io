For over two centuries, crystallographers swore that five-fold rotational symmetry was mathematically impossible in nature: a flat floor can be tiled with triangles, squares, or hexagons, but never pentagons without leaving gaps. In 1974, Oxford physicist Sir Roger Penrose shattered this dogma using just two simple golden rhombuses—thick and thin. He discovered a mathematical miracle: an aperiodic tiling that tiles the infinite universe with local 5-fold and 10-fold symmetry, yet never repeats a single translational motif.

The Quasicrystal & Penrose Tiling Lexicon

The Crystallographic Restriction Theorem

In standard periodic crystal lattices, spatial translation symmetry strictly limits rotational symmetries to 2-fold, 3-fold, 4-fold, or 6-fold ($n \in \{2, 3, 4, 6\}$). Five-fold rotational symmetry ($\theta = 72^\circ$) was long proven to be impossible for any repeating Euclidean lattice.

Robinson Triangle Deflation

The Penrose P3 tiling is decomposed into two isosceles Robinson triangles: acute Golden Triangles ($36^\circ, 72^\circ, 72^\circ$) and obtuse Golden Gnomons ($108^\circ, 36^\circ, 36^\circ$). Under recursive subdivision, every triangle deflates by the Golden Ratio $\phi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.6180339887$, spawning self-similar infinite patterns.

The Golden Ratio Asymptote ($\phi$)

In any finite patch of Penrose tiling, let $N_{\text{thick}}$ and $N_{\text{thin}}$ be the counts of thick and thin rhombuses. As the area approaches infinity, their ratio converges strictly to the Golden Ratio: $\lim_{A \to \infty} \frac{N_{\text{thick}}}{N_{\text{thin}}} = \phi$.

Dan Shechtman's Quasicrystals (2011 Nobel Prize)

In 1982, Israeli materials scientist Dan Shechtman observed sharp 10-fold electron diffraction peaks in a rapidly quenched aluminum-manganese alloy ($Al_6Mn$). Mocked initially by Linus Pauling, Shechtman was awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry when Penrose tiling explained real physical quasicrystals.
